## Appendix B — Scoring rubric

Each privacy policy was scored 0–10 on each of the seven data-protection principles
of GDPR Article 5(1), using the criteria below. Scoring was applied with
large-language-model assistance under author review; the limitations of this
instrument are set out in §2.4. For each principle, the score reflects how fully and
specifically the policy addresses the questions listed, with vague, manipulative, or
overly broad language penalised (for example, "we may use your data for a variety of
purposes" without specification).

**1. Lawfulness, fairness and transparency** — Does the policy establish that access
is granted voluntarily and identify a lawful basis for processing? By what mechanism
is consent obtained (form, checkbox, survey), and can the site be used without
agreeing to collection? What options does the consent interface present? Where the
policy appears machine-generated, does it nonetheless accurately reflect the site's
actual processing?
*Indicative terms: consent, agree, lawful basis, voluntarily, legitimate interest, form,
checkbox.*

**2. Purpose limitation** — Does the policy state the specific purposes for which data
is collected and used, and are those purposes distinguished (the operator's own use,
user-experience purposes, and sharing with partners) rather than blended into a
single open-ended list?
*Indicative terms: purpose, in order to, how we use your data, for the following
reasons.*

**3. Data minimisation** — Is collection limited to what the stated purpose requires?
What categories of data are collected, and does the policy justify each against a
purpose? Does the described collection support profiling or targeted advertising
beyond what the service needs to function?
*Indicative terms: data we collect, categories of personal data, necessary for,
profiling, targeted.*

**4. Accuracy** — Does the policy address the obligation under Article 5(1)(d) to keep
personal data accurate and, where necessary, up to date? Specifically: does it give the
data subject a means to review, correct, or update their personal data; does it commit
to correcting or erasing inaccurate data; and does it describe any process for keeping
records current? Policies that provide a clear rectification route and state a commitment
to accuracy score highest; those that omit rectification entirely, or mention it only as a
generic legal-rights list without a usable mechanism, score lowest.
*Indicative terms: accurate, up to date, correct, rectify, rectification, update your
information, keep your data current.*

**5. Storage limitation** — Does the policy state how long personal data is retained,
and is the stated period specific and justified rather than indefinite or open-ended
("as long as necessary" without qualification)? Does it describe what happens to data
at the end of the retention period?
*Indicative terms: retention, retained for, how long, delete, stored until, data
lifecycle.*

**6. Integrity and confidentiality** — Does the policy describe the security measures
protecting personal data (technical, organisational, or both), and does it describe how
data is handled after the retention period? Is this stated with enough specificity to be
meaningful, or only as a generic assurance?
*Indicative terms: secure, encrypted, security measures, access control, safeguards,
confidentiality.*

**7. Accountability** — Does the policy identify who is responsible for the data — a
named controller, and a Data Protection Officer contact where one is required (or an
evident basis for exemption)? Does it state the concrete actions a data subject must
take to exercise their rights (access, deletion, complaint), rather than merely asserting
that such rights exist?
*Indicative terms: Data Protection Officer, DPO, controller, contact, your rights, to
exercise, supervisory authority, lodge a complaint.*

---

*Note on the accuracy principle (§8): accuracy is the lowest-scoring principle in the
corpus, with 43 of 89 sites scoring below 7. The criteria above were applied uniformly
across all sites; the low scores reflect that rectification mechanisms and explicit
accuracy commitments are frequently absent or buried in a generic rights list, not a
gap in how the principle was assessed.*
